Every store has a corner of products that stopped selling. They don't show up as a problem on any single day, which is exactly why they pile up. Dead stock is cash you've already spent, sitting on a shelf, often still being reordered out of habit.

What counts as dead stock

A practical definition: items in stock that haven't sold in 90 days. Adjust the window to your business. A fashion store might use 60 days; a hardware store with slow but steady lines might use 180. The point is to pick a number and look at the list regularly.

Close behind it is overstock: items that do sell, but slowly enough that you hold far more than you need. Six months of stock or more is a common warning level.

Step 1: Find it and put a number on it

For each item, you need its stock, its cost per unit and the date of its last sale. Multiply stock by cost to see the money tied up. Sort by that, largest first. Ten minutes with this list is usually an eye-opener: a handful of items often hold most of the value.

Use cost, not retail price. A $40 lamp that cost you $30 ties up $30 of cash, and that's the figure that matters when deciding how hard to discount.

Step 2: Find out why it stopped selling

  • It's hidden. Not in any collection, poor photos, out of search. Fix the listing before you discount.
  • The season passed. It'll sell again next year; consider whether storing it costs less than discounting it.
  • A variant nobody wants. The size or colour that never sells. Stop buying it in the first place.
  • It's been replaced by a newer product, by you or the market.

Step 3: Clear it, cheapest option first

  1. Return it to the supplier, if your terms allow, even with a restocking fee.
  2. Bundle it with a best seller. The bundle moves the slow item without discounting the fast one on its own.
  3. Discount it in steps: a modest markdown first, deeper only if needed. Recovering your cost is a win; anything above it is a bonus.
  4. Clear it out through a clearance sale, outlet or donation, when the space and cash are worth more than the stock.

Step 4: Stop buying more of it

This is the step that's easiest to forget. If an item is on a standing order, or gets added to purchase orders by habit, mark it as "don't reorder" the moment you decide to clear it. Then make sure your reorder suggestions are based on actual recent sales, so a slow item's suggested quantity falls to zero on its own.

One caution: an item that shows no sales because it was out of stock isn't dead, it's missing. Look at whether it was available before writing it off.

Make it a monthly habit

Dead stock is much cheaper to deal with early. A short monthly look at the list (what's new on it, what's been there for a while, and how much cash it holds) keeps it from quietly growing.
